Physics Course · Theory the number first — the formula after

Motion with constant acceleration

or uniformly accelerated motion

Acceleration
means how fast velocity is changing. a vector — it has a size and a direction, just like velocity itself.
Uniformly
means that acceleration is not changing with time. the same amount is added every second, for as long as the motion lasts.
1

What the number is telling you

Try to focus only on the numbers for now — the vectors come next.

Suppose we are told that a body moves in a straight line and speeds up with acceleration a = 15 m/s². What does that actually tell us?

It tells us one thing: with each second that passes, the speed grows by 15 m/s.

Now the opposite case. A body moving to the right, starting at 50 m/s, slowing down with the same number — a = 15 m/s².

Either way, the number says the same thing — by how much the speed changes in one second. Nothing more than that.

2

The full definition

You would not be wrong to memorise that acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Now let's open that sentence up.
The definition
a = ΔvΔt
change of velocity, divided by the time it took.
The change itself
Δv = v − u
v — the velocity at the end, u — the velocity at the start.

Put one into the other and rearrange:

a = v − ut ⇒ v = u + a·t
Before we use it — look at these four: u, v, t, a. Which of them change as the motion goes on, and which stay put?

t is the one that runs — it is the variable. a stays fixed, because uniformly accelerated is exactly what that word promised. u stays fixed too — it was set once, at the start, and the past does not change. So only v moves, and it moves because time moves.

v(t) = u + a·t now I can see who is who

Let's play with it

t = 0 s  →  v(0) = u + a·0 = u of course — nothing has happened yet
t = 1 s  →  v(1) = u + a one second, one whole vector a added on
t = 2 s  →  v(2) = u + 2a and now one more

First example — the vectors agree

You simply add the acceleration vector onto the velocity — once every second.

Here u and a point the same way, so every second makes the velocity longer. The body speeds up.

Second example — the vectors disagree

Now they point against each other. The velocity shrinks — 10, 8, 6 — reaches zero at 5 seconds, and then keeps going: by 10 seconds the body is moving at 10 m/s again, the other way.

The acceleration never changed. Not at the start, not at the moment the body stopped. Only the velocity did.

Watch out!

An acceleration vector is never negative. A vector has a length and a direction — "negative" is not one of the things it can be. The minus signs you are about to meet belong to the next section, where we project onto an axis.

3

The same motion, seen from an axis

Same two examples — nothing about the physics changes. The only new thing is an x-axis, which says which direction we have decided to call positive.

The axis points left, and the body travels right. So every projection comes out negative — even though the body is speeding up the whole time.

Vector form
v(t) = u + a·t
what we used above — arrows, no signs.
Seen from the axis
vx(t) = ux + ax·t
the same sentence, written in numbers that carry a sign.

Does it hold up?

From the picture: ux = −5 m/s and ax = −10 m/s². Put them in:

vx(0) = −5 − 10·0 = −5 m/s✓
vx(1) = −5 − 10·1 = −15 m/s✓
vx(2) = −5 − 10·2 = −25 m/s✓

Every number came out negative — and the body was speeding up the entire time.

And the second example

This time the axis points right — along the initial velocity, against the acceleration. So ux is positive and ax is negative:

What the axis reads
ux = 10 m/s
ax = −2 m/s²
And the velocity goes
t=0 → 10   t=1 → 8   t=2 → 6
t=5 → 0   t=9 → −8   t=10 → −10 m/s

The same formula, and it fits every line.

Watch out!

A negative ax does not mean "slowing down". It means the velocity projection is getting smaller. Whether the body speeds up or slows down depends on something else — whether ax and vx carry the same sign. In the first example both were negative, and the body was gaining speed all the way.

4

The same formula, seen by a mathematician

Look again at what we ended up with — and then look at something you already know from maths.

vx(t) = ux + ax·t
y = c + m·x
the same sentence twice — only the letters were swapped.
What changes — the variables
vx → y
t → x
What stays — the constants
ux → c
ax → m

And from maths we already know everything about y = c + mx: it draws a straight line, c is where it meets the axis, and m is how steeply it climbs.

So if the two formulas are the same formula — then a vx(t) graph has to be a straight line too. As long as ax stays constant.

Four signs, four lines

Each pair below is the same motion told twice — the arrows on the left, the graph on the right. The axis points right every time, so the signs come straight off the picture.

ux > 0  ·  ax > 0
starts positive, climbs. Speeding up.
ux < 0  ·  ax > 0
starts negative, crosses zero, keeps climbing.
ux > 0  ·  ax < 0
slows, stops, then runs back the other way.
ux < 0  ·  ax < 0
negative and getting more so. Also speeding up.

The sign of ax tilts the line. The sign of ux only says where it starts.

5

Finding the displacement

Let us build an example and keep it for the whole section. Positive direction to the right; the body also moves right, starting at 5 m/s; it speeds up, and ten seconds later it is doing 35 m/s. The motion is uniformly accelerated — but we are not told the acceleration, and we will not need it.

That is how we see it. A physicist draws the same thing differently — as a straight line, exactly as section 4 promised:

So — how far did it travel in those ten seconds? Three ways in, all arriving at the same place.

Way 1 — swap it for an easier trip

From the previous topic we know the idea of average velocity: when a trip is not tidy, we may replace it with an imaginary one that takes the same time, covers the same distance, and does it at a constant velocity.

And when velocity changes at a steady rate, that average is simply the middle of the two numbers:

vaver,x = ux + vx2 = 5 + 352 = 20 m/s

it always lands exactly halfway between the two velocities

Now the green trip is easy — constant velocity, and we already know what to do with those:

Sx = vaver,x · t Sx = 20 · 10 = 200 m
Way 2 — the area under the graph

Also from the previous topic: the area under a v(t) graph is the displacement. Here that area is a trapezium — parallel sides ux and vx, width t.

Area = ux + vx2 · t the trapezium rule, straight from maths.

Which is the formula we already had — arrived at without ever mentioning an average.

Way 3 — why those two are the same picture

Way 1 measured the green rectangle. Way 2 measured the area under the red line. They gave the same number, and that is not a coincidence — look:

→

The piece sticking out above the red line fits exactly into the gap below it — because the two lines cross at the halfway point. Same area, rearranged.

So the first displacement formula for uniformly accelerated motion, by whichever road you reached it:

Sx = ux + vx2 · t no acceleration anywhere in it — which is exactly why it was usable before we knew a.
Watch out!

This is average velocity, not average speed, and the halfway rule only works because the acceleration is constant. If the body turns around during the trip, the distance it covers is larger than Sx — the displacement only compares the two ends.

6

Two more displacement formulas

We now hold two statements about this motion:

from section 2
vx = ux + ax·t
call it (∗)
from section 5
Sx = ux + vx2 · t
call it (1)

Formula (1) works with the combination ux, vx, t. But a problem does not always hand you those three. So let us make the same statement say different things.

Swap vx out

Take (∗) and drop it into (1), in place of vx:

Sx = ux + ux + axt2 · t  =  (2ux + axt)t2
Sx = ux·t + axt²2 (2) — a new combination: ux, ax, t. No vx needed.

Or swap t out

Rearrange (∗) for time, and put that into (1):

t = vx − uxax  ⇒  Sx = ux + vx2 · vx − uxax

— and the top is a difference of squares waiting to happen.

Sx = vx² − ux²2ax (3) — combination vx, ux, ax. No time in it at all.

Three doors into the same room

(1) Sx = ux + vx2 · t you know ux, vx, t (2) Sx = uxt + axt²2 you know ux, ax, t (3) Sx = vx² − ux²2ax you know vx, ux, ax

Which one? Read the problem and ask what you were actually given. The right formula is the one whose three letters you already hold — it is never about which looks nicer.

7

Displacement and time, seen by a mathematician

Section 4 did this for velocity, and the line turned out straight. Now the same trick on formula (2):

Sx = ux·t + ax2·t²
y = b·x + a·x²
a quadratic — the same one you have plotted a hundred times in maths.
What changes
Sx → y
t → x
What stays
ux → b
ax/2 → a
an Sx(t) graph is a parabola whenever the motion is uniformly accelerated.
There is no third term here — no "+ c" — only because we are plotting displacement, which starts at zero by definition. Plot the coordinate instead and that c reappears: it is x₀, where the body was when the clock started.

Example 1 — same start, opposite pushes

Two bodies both begin at rest, u = 0. They are pushed with the same acceleration, 1 m/s², but in opposite directions. The axis points right.

body 1
u1x = 0 m/s
a1x = +1 m/s²
S1x(t) = 0·t + 1·t²2 = 0.5t²
body 2
u2x = 0 m/s
a2x = −1 m/s²
S2x(t) = 0·t + (−1)·t²2 = −0.5t²

Same shape, flipped. Changing the direction of a flips the parabola — nothing else about it moves.

Example 2 — same speed, opposite ways, both braking

Now a harder pair. Two bodies set off at the same speed, 10 m/s, in opposite directions, and each one is braking at 2 m/s². Everything about them is identical except which way they are facing. The axis still points right.

Body 1 travels right, so its velocity projection is positive — and it brakes, so its acceleration projection is negative. Body 2 is the exact mirror: both signs flip.

body 1 — moving right
u1x = +10 m/s
a1x = −2 m/s²
S1x(t) = 10t + (−2)t²2 = 10t − t²
body 2 — moving left
u2x = −10 m/s
a2x = +2 m/s²
S2x(t) = −10t + 2t²2 = −10t + t²

These two expressions are each other with a minus sign in front. So the graphs must be mirror images across the time axis — and this time the parabolas turn around:

Read the red curve as a story. It climbs for five seconds, reaching 25 m — and that peak is exactly the moment the body stops, because the slope of an S(t) graph is velocity, and at the top the slope is zero. Then it comes back down. At ten seconds the curve is at zero again: the body is home.

Watch out!

At t = 10 s the graph reads Sx = 0 — but the body did not stand still for ten seconds. It went 25 m out and 25 m back: 50 m of path, zero displacement. The graph only ever tells you where the body ended up relative to where it began, and that is the first thing you learned about displacement.

Two bodies, one description. The only thing that differed was a direction — and the whole of it came out as a pair of signs.

8

From displacement to coordinate

Everything so far has answered one question: how far has the body moved from where it started? That is displacement, and it always begins at zero, because at t = 0 nothing has happened yet.

But a problem rarely asks that. It asks where the body is — at the 40-metre mark, past the gate, three metres short of the wall. And to answer that we need one more piece of information that displacement never carried: where the body was when the clock started.

Call it x0. Then the position at any moment is simply where it began, plus how far it has moved since:

x(t) = x0 + Sx(t)

and since we already know what Sx is:

x(t) = x0 + uxt + axt²2 the equation of motion — it tells you the body's position at any moment you care to name.
You have met this shape before. In the constant-velocity topic it was x = x₀ + vxt — start here, add how far you have gone. The acceleration has only added one more term to the end of that same sentence.

And notice what x0 is, in the language of section 7: it is the "+ c" that was missing from the quadratic. It was missing because displacement starts at zero. Position does not.

The first pair again

Two bodies from rest, accelerations of 1 m/s² in opposite directions — nothing about them changes. We only add where each one was standing: body 1 at the 4 m mark, body 2 at 40 m.

body 1
x1(t) = 4 + 0·t + 1·t²2 = 4 + 0.5t²
body 2
x2(t) = 40 + 0·t + (−1)·t²2 = 40 − 0.5t²

Put the two graphs next to each other. On the left, what we drew in section 7. On the right, the same motion written as position:

displacement

both start at zero

coordinate

each starts where it stood

Same two curves. Same curvature, same direction of bend, same everything — one has been lifted to start at 4, the other to start at 40. x0 does not touch the shape. It only decides the height at t = 0.

And here is what the right-hand graph can tell you that the left one never could: the two curves cross. At that moment the bodies have the same coordinate — they are in the same place. A displacement graph can never show you that, because each body measures from its own starting line.

And the braking pair

Same again with example 2 — both at 10 m/s, opposite ways, both braking at 2 m/s². Body 1 starts at the 20 m mark, body 2 at 80 m.

body 1
x1(t) = 20 + 10t − t²
body 2
x2(t) = 80 − 10t + t²

A hill and a valley. Body 1 climbs from 20 m to 45 m, stops at five seconds, and comes back down to where it began. Body 2 does the mirror of that, from 80 m down to 55 m and back up. They never meet — at their closest they are still ten metres apart.

Nothing new happened here. The same two parabolas from the last section, each one lifted to start where its body actually stood.

Watch out!

Do not read a rising x(t) graph as "going up". The axis is a line on the ground, not a height. Rising means the coordinate is growing — the body is moving towards the positive end of the axis, whichever way you pointed it.
