Maths for Physicsthe tools we keep reaching for

Physics speaks in maths. Not because physicists love suffering, but because maths is the shortest way to say how much and how it changes.

This card is not a maths course. It is a toolbox: only the things we really use when we solve physics problems. Come back here every time a problem gets stuck on the maths, not on the physics.

1

Numbers. What is inside them?

I want you to think about how much information a number gives us. And the answer is — not much! A number is just a quantity. It tells you how much, and nothing else.

The mass of an object is 5 kg.

Here 5 is the number of kilograms — one piece of information: how much of something. The unit kg is the second piece: of what.

5 kg literally means 5 × (one kilogram). Remember this — it will save you in section 7!

So in physics a number never walks alone. "5" — five what? Apples? Metres? Seconds?

2

Equation

All equations have a left side and a right side, and an "=" sign between them. In other words, something that represents the left side is equal to something that represents the right side.

Example. Dima has 5 apples. Pasha has 5 apples too.

So we write 5 = 5. And it is actually very difficult to say from this equation which "5" is whose. That is why physicists like to use letters first — so we always know what we are talking about.

So, instead of5 = 5 we writeD = P D — Dima's apples, P — Pasha's apples
3

What can we do with an equation?

Let's come back to the same example with Dima and Pasha. What if we add two more apples to Dima?

Only Dima got apples
D ≠ P5+2 ≠ 5not equal anymore!
Both got apples
D = P5+2 = 5+27 = 7equal again

From this we see that the left side is not equal to the right side anymore. To return to the equality, we have to do the same thing with Pasha — also add two more.

This is going to work with all mathematical operations!

The only rule you really need

If you do something with the left side of an equation, do exactly the same with the right side — then the equation keeps working.

The quick way you will see in books

Very soon you will stop writing "+3 to both sides" every time. People say: "move the 3 to the other side and change its sign". It is not a new rule — it is our rule, done fast:

  • a term (something added or subtracted) jumps to the other side with the opposite sign: a − 3 = 7 → a = 7 + 3;
  • a multiplier jumps to the other side as a divisor, and a divisor as a multiplier;
  • if each side is one single fraction, you can multiply "crosswise": ab = cd → a·d = b·c.
Where you'll meet it — every single problem

Speed: v = st. The problem asks for the time. Find t:

v·t = st·tI don't like t downstairs — multiply both sides by t vt = s vtv = svnow divide both sides by v t = sv
Watch out!

The "−1" flip works only when each side is one single fraction. From 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 you can not get R = R1 + R2. First add the right side into one fraction, then flip.

A square root gives two answers, + and −. In physics you then choose with your head: a length or a time of −1.69 usually makes no sense, but a velocity of −1.69 m/s can be perfectly fine.

4

What is a function?

It is a bit hard to find the right words to describe this, so let's look at how people use this word in real life.

Refrigerator
Can you tell me the function of a refrigerator? Easy! It takes our food and makes it cold enough to keep it fresh as long as possible.
Cow
The function of a cow? Easy! You give her grass and water, and she gives you milk — and from it we get cheese, sour cream, cottage cheese and butter.
Microwave
You give it frozen or cold food — it gives you a warm, ready-to-eat meal.

Conclusion from these examples: a function takes something (the input), does something with it, and gives us back the result (the output). And in maths it is the same!

General form of a function:y = f(x)

With words: this function takes an x-value, does something with it — something that is written inside this function — and gives us the result as a y-value.

Example: y = x + 1

It is a defined function, so I see what it does with x: it adds one. Let's see how it works! We just need to put some numbers instead of x:

Input xWhat the function doesOutput yPoint
x = 0y = 0 + 11A (0, 1)
x = 1y = 1 + 12B (1, 2)
x = 2y = 2 + 13C (2, 3)

When a function works, it always produces y's that are connected to some x's. That's why we always put x and y together, as a pair: (x, y). And a pair is a point — so we can also show this function visually!

But what if I put lots and lots of x's inside the function? We are going to have an infinite number of points. And if I put them on the graph, I am going to see a straight line.

5

Straight-line function

y = m x + c— general form

But let us get the understanding of this form slowly, starting from the most famous straight line: y = x, which is the same as y = x1.

If the maximum power of x is one, the graph is going to be a straight line — so we need just two points to draw it! For y = x: x = 1 gives y = 1, so A (1, 1); x = 2 gives y = 2, so B (2, 2). Connect them — done.

Two points are enough for a line. A third one is just to check yourself.

The "c" coefficient

Now, let's understand the c coefficient in the standard form. We already know y = x. Let's build two more lines the same way — put two x-values, 0 and 1, inside each function and see which y's come out:

y = x + 1
x = 0 → y = 0 + 1 = 1(0, 1)x = 1 → y = 1 + 1 = 2(1, 2)
y = x − 1
x = 0 → y = 0 − 1 = −1(0, −1)x = 1 → y = 1 − 1 = 0(1, 0)

Two points for each line — that is enough to draw it. Now put all three lines on one graph:

From these examples we get that the c coefficient shows us by how much the line is lifted up or dropped down. Or, in other words, at which coordinate on the y-axis the line is going through. So that is why it is called the y-intercept.

The "m" coefficient

Now let's understand the m coefficient. This time I keep c = 0 and only change m:

Look what happens: with m = 2 the line climbs fast, with m = ½ it climbs slowly, and with m = −1 it goes down. So m shows how steep the line is — that is why it is called the slope (or gradient).

But "steep" is just a feeling. Let's turn it into a number. Take any two points on the line and ask: when I walk one step to the right, how much do I climb?

Where you'll meet it — motion with constant velocity

Here comes the payoff. The coordinate equation from motion with constant velocity is exactly a straight line — just with physics letters:

y = c + m x → x = x0 + vt

The y-intercept is where the body was when we turned on the stopwatch (x0). The slope is the velocity v. Same maths, now with meaning.

Watch out!

The slope is not "how high the line is". A line can sit very high on the graph and be completely flat (m = 0) — a body standing still far from the origin.

And always compute the slope with the numbers on the axes, with units — not by measuring the picture with a protractor. Stretch the axes and the same line looks steeper.

6

Two equations at once

Sometimes one equation is not enough, because there are two unknowns. Then we need two equations — and we solve them together. This is called a system.

x + y = 10x − y = 4the brace means "both are true at the same time"

Way 1 — substitution

Express one letter from one equation, and put it inside the other one. Now the second equation has only one unknown — and we already know how to deal with that.

Way 2 — adding the equations

Equations can be added like the two sides of a scale: left + left = right + right. Choose it so that one letter disappears.

Substitution
y = 2xx + y = 9 x + 2x = 9put 2x instead of y 3x = 9 → x = 3 x = 3, y = 6
Adding
x + y = 10x − y = 4+y and −y will cancel 2x = 14 → x = 7added left to left, right to right x = 7, y = 3
Where you'll meet it — "when and where do they meet?"

Two towns are 240 km apart. Car A leaves the first town at 60 km/h; at the same moment car B leaves the second town towards it at 20 km/h. Both coordinates are measured from the first town:

xA = 60txB = 240 − 20t 60t = 240 − 20tthey meet ⇒ same x 80t = 240+20t to both sides t = 3 h, x = 180 km

On the graph a system is just the point where two lines cross.

Powers of ten and units

Physics jumps between the tiny and the huge: an atom is about 0.000 000 000 1 m, the Sun is 150 000 000 000 m away from us. Counting zeros is painful and dangerous, so we write such numbers with powers of ten:

150 000 000 000 m = 1.5 · 1011 m 0.000 000 000 1 m = 1 · 10−10 m

The power just says how many steps the decimal point jumps: positive — big number, negative — small number.

8

The right triangle

A right triangle has one angle of 90°. The longest side, opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse. The other two sides are named by where they stand relative to the angle α we are looking at: one is opposite to it, one is adjacent (lying next to it).

Pythagoras

c² = a² + b²3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5²

Sine, cosine, tangent

These are just names for the ratios of the sides. For a given angle the ratio is always the same — however big you draw the triangle.

sin α = oppositehypotenuse cos α = adjacenthypotenuse tan α = oppositeadjacent = sin αcos α

SOH · CAH · TOA — say it out loud a few times, it sticks.

For our 3-4-5 triangle: sin α = 3/5 = 0.6, cos α = 4/5 = 0.8, tan α = 3/4 = 0.75. And to get the angle back from a ratio, use the inverse button: α = tan−1(0.75) ≈ 37°.

Because of Pythagoras, there is one identity that is always true: sin²α + cos²α = 1.

Values worth knowing by heart

Watch out!

"Opposite" and "adjacent" depend on which angle you look from. Switch to the other sharp angle and they swap places.

Check that your calculator is in degrees (DEG) when the angle is in degrees. sin 30 in radian mode gives −0.99 — and a very confusing evening.

9

Vectors and their components

A vector is an arrow: it has a size (its length, also called magnitude or modulus) and a direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force — all of them are vectors. A number like mass or time has no direction; it is called a scalar.

Arrows pointing in a slanted direction are awkward to work with. So we do a trick: we look at the arrow's shadows on the axes. Shine a light from above — the shadow on the x-axis is the x-component. Shine from the side — the shadow on the y-axis is the y-component.

The vector and its two components make a right triangle — so section 8 does all the work:

vx = v cos α vy = v sin α v = √(vx² + vy²) tan α = vyvx

Here α is measured from the x-axis. Measure it from the y-axis and sin and cos swap — always look at your own drawing.

Along an axis
the component equals the whole vector (with + or −)
Across an axis
(perpendicular) the component on that axis is zero — no shadow at all

Adding vectors

Vectors are not added like plain numbers: 3 m/s and 4 m/s can give anything from 1 m/s to 7 m/s, depending on the directions. There are two pictures for it — they always give the same answer:

The easiest way to calculate a sum is with components: add all the x-components, add all the y-components, then put the result back together with Pythagoras.

Watch out!

A component can be negative — the size of a vector can not. "The velocity is −3 m/s" means "3 m/s, pointing against the axis".

The circle and the radian

L = 2πRcircumference — one full lap S = πR²area

π ≈ 3.14 is how many diameters fit around a circle — for any circle, from a coin to the Earth's orbit.

Measuring angles with a ruler

Degrees are a human invention: someone once decided that a full turn is 360. Physics prefers a more natural way: measure the angle by how long the arc is, compared to the radius.

θ = sRangle in radians

A full lap: s = 2πR, so θ = 2π. That means

360° = 2π rad180° = π rad

1 rad ≈ 57°. The arc length is then simply s = Rθ.

Where you'll meet it — distance vs displacement on a circle
s = 2πR4 = π m ≈ 3.14 ma quarter of a lap |Δr| = √(R² + R²) = R√2Pythagoras! ≈ 2.83 m

After a full lap the path is 2πR, but the displacement is zero — you are back where you started.

Watch out!

θ = s/R and s = Rθ work only in radians. Put 90 instead of π/2 and the arc comes out 57 times too long.

The parabola and the quadratic equation

Remember the red rule from section 5: maximum power of x is one — straight line. Now let the maximum power be two:

y = ax² + bx + c— a parabola
a > 0
branches go up, like a smile — the vertex is the lowest point
a < 0
branches go down, like a frown — the vertex is the highest point
|a| big
narrow parabola; |a| small — wide
c
the y-intercept again, just like in a line

Two points of a parabola matter most of all: the vertex (the turning point) and the roots — the x's where y = 0, where the parabola crosses the x-axis.

xvertex = −b2a D = b² − 4ac x1,2 = −b ± √D2a

D > 0 — two roots, D = 0 — one root (the vertex touches the axis), D < 0 — no roots: the parabola never reaches y = 0.

y = x² − 4x + 3
xv = −−42·1 = 2 yv = 4 − 8 + 3 = −1put x = 2 inside D = 16 − 12 = 4 x = 4 ± 22 x1 = 1, x2 = 3
Where you'll meet it — accelerated motion

A ball is thrown straight up at 20 m/s. With g ≈ 10 m/s², its height is h = 20t − 5t² — a parabola with a < 0, a frown.

When is it 15 m high?
15 = 20t − 5t² t² − 4t + 3 = 0everything to one side, ÷5 t1 = 1 s, t2 = 3 sour example above!

Both answers are true: once on the way up, once on the way down. The vertex is the highest point: t = −20 / (2·(−5)) = 2 s, h = 40 − 20 = 20 m.

Watch out!

Don't throw away a root automatically — ask what it means. If the ball is thrown from a cliff and we ask when it is 25 m below the edge, we get t = 5 s and t = −1 s.

The −1 s is not "an error". Remember: t = 0 is just the moment we turned on the stopwatch. −1 s answers the question "if the ball had been flying on this same parabola before we started watching, when would it have been there?" It didn't — so for our problem only 5 s is the answer.
