Projectile motion

or what happens when you throw something into the air

Throw something into the air and watch it. A ball, a stone, a set of keys across the room. It leaves your hand, it curves, it comes down. Nothing is pushing it, nothing is steering it — and yet it traces out a shape so precise that you could have drawn it beforehand.

Through this whole topic we make one agreement: there is no air resistance. Once the object leaves the hand, only gravity works on it. That is the entire physical setup. Everything else in this card is just bookkeeping.

Free fall — read both words

Fall is the easy half: the thing is going down, or will be going down shortly. Free is the half that carries the physics — it means nothing is acting on the body except gravitational attraction. No air, no hand, no string.

And gravity has a habit worth memorising right now: g always points vertically downwards. Not along the path, not backwards along the throw. Straight down, always, no matter which way the body happens to be flying at that moment.

✎
Keep this arrow in your head for the rest of the card. It is the same arrow at every single point of the flight — and every section below leans on exactly that.

Two scenarios are waiting for us. One is simple. One is not that simple — and the whole art of this card is showing you that the second one is just the first one, done twice.

1 The simple scenario — straight up and down

Start with the case where the body never leaves a single vertical line. You drop it. You throw it straight up and it comes back. You throw it up from a balcony and it sails past you on the way down.

Different stories, one and the same physics

All of these are just uniformly accelerated motion along a straight line — exactly the thing we built in the previous topic. The line happens to be vertical and the acceleration happens to be g, but nothing new is going on. You can apply every formula we already have.

Try it on a real one

A ball is thrown vertically upwards with u = 15 m/s from the ground, yi = 0. At the very same moment, a very deep hole opens up right beneath it, so the ball is free to keep going past the ground.

Find the displacement Sy and the velocity vy at t = 1 s, 1.5 s, 3 s and 4 s.

The ground opens at the moment of the throw — nothing stops the ball on its way back down

What we know

uy = +15 m/s

ay = −g = −10 m/s²

yi = 0

What we choose

vy = uy + ayt

Sy = uyt + ayt²/2

Why these two? Look at what we already hold in our hands: uy, ay and t. Formulas (∗) and (1) from the previous topic are built from exactly these three — so they are the ones we take.

Read the last column again rather than the numbers. At t = 3 s the displacement is zero while the speed is 15 m/s — the ball is moving fast and has got nowhere. And the velocity at 3 s is the exact mirror of the velocity at the start: same size, opposite sign. The motion up and the motion down are the same motion, read in opposite directions.

Watch out!

At the top, vy = 0 — but ay is still −10 m/s². The ball is not "weightless" for an instant and it does not pause. Velocity passing through zero says nothing at all about acceleration.

2 The not-so-simple fall

Now throw the ball at an angle instead of straight up. The path stops being a line and becomes a curve — an ideal parabola.

And here is the thing worth noticing before any algebra: when you throw something, you only control two numbers. How hard you throw it — the initial speed u — and which way you aim — the angle α. Everything else about that parabola is decided for you by gravity.

Two dials, and the parabola does the rest

3 Two people watching the same throw

Here is the general problem we want to crack. We are given u, α and g. We want the time up, the time down, the total time, the range S along the ground and the maximum height H.

It looks hard, and for an honest reason: the velocity is changing direction and magnitude at every single point of the flight. There is no one number to hold on to.

So how do we deal with that? Easy — we just need two spectators. Samir and Eva will help us. We send Samir up in a hot-air balloon to watch the throw from above. Eva stands far off to the right, exactly on the line of the throw, and watches from there.

Samir looks straight down on the flight. Eva looks along the line of the throw.

Many thanks to our researchers, Samir and Eva. Now it is time to explain their results.

Neither of them is wrong, and neither of them saw a curve. Samir, looking straight down, could only see the horizontal part of the motion. Eva, looking along the line of the throw, could only see the vertical part. The parabola only exists when you put their two reports back together.

Why each of them saw what they saw

Gravity points straight down. Project it onto the two axes and the answer falls out:

Horizontal

gx = 0 m/s²

nothing accelerates the ball sideways — so Samir saw constant speed

Vertical

gy = −g ≈ −9.81 m/s²

the full pull acts here — so Eva saw speed changing

Which gives us the idea the whole topic rests on: split one complicated motion into two straight-line motions, one along x and one along y. Horizontal: motion with constant speed. Vertical: uniformly accelerated motion. Both of them we already know how to do.

4 Breaking the velocity apart

If we are going to run two motions side by side, then at every moment the ball needs two velocities — one for each of them.

Green never changes length. Blue shrinks, vanishes, and grows back the other way.

Written down, the picture says:

at any moment v⃗ = vx⃗ + vy⃗,    so    v² = vx² + vy²

The second form is just Pythagoras — the components sit at a right angle, so the actual speed is the hypotenuse. And three facts fall straight out of the drawing:

  • vx is the same at every point of the flight, start to finish.
  • vy shrinks on the way up, hits zero at the top, and grows on the way down.
  • The motion is symmetrical: the ball lands with the same speed it was launched with, mirrored.

Watch out!

Eva wrote that the ball stopped at B — and from where she stood, it did: its vertical motion stopped. Samir saw no stop at all. At the top, vy = 0 — but v ≠ 0. The ball is still moving at vx, horizontally, as fast as it ever was. "Highest point" never means "stopped".

5 Two motions, two sets of formulas

So now we can say it cleanly:

  • Horizontal motion is motion with constant speed.
  • Vertical motion is uniformly accelerated motion.

Which means every single formula from the two previous topics is available to us — we just have to remember which axis we are standing on when we use it.

Horizontal — constant speed

x = x0 + Sx

Sx = vx · t

Vertical — constant acceleration

(∗) vy(t) = uy + ayt

(1) Sy = uyt + ayt²/2

(2) Sy = (uy + vy)t / 2

(3) Sy = (vy² − uy²) / (2ay)

✎
Notice there is nothing new on this page. Not one formula here was invented for projectiles — they are all borrowed. That is the whole trick of the topic.

6 Finding the times

Given u, α and g — find tup, tdown and ttotal.

A is the launch, B is the highest point — and at B the vertical story ends

Step one — split the launch velocity

uy = u · sin α

vx = u · cos α

Now the two motions can be dealt with separately, and each of them is one we already know.

Step two — run the vertical motion from A to B

We know uy, we know vy = 0 at the top, and we know ay = −g.

So use (∗):   0 = u sin α − g·tup

time up equals time down tup = u sin α / g = tdown   ⟹   ttotal = 2tup = 2u sin α / g

Notice what the answer does not contain: cos α. The horizontal half of the throw has no say whatsoever in how long the ball stays in the air. Only how steeply you aimed.

7 Finding the displacements — x and y

We have the times. Now the distances: the range Sx along the ground, and the maximum height H.

The range — from the horizontal motion

Horizontally there is nothing but constant speed, so the range is speed times time — and we already know both.

Sx = vx · ttotal = u cos α · 2u sin α / g

And here mathematics hands us a present. That combination 2 sin α cos α is exactly sin 2α. So:

range Sx = u² sin(2α) / g
✎
Notice! sin(2α) is biggest when 2α = 90°, which means α = 45°. That is why 45° throws the furthest — and now you know it is a fact about a sine function, not about balls.

The maximum height — from the vertical motion

For the height we go back to the vertical story: from the launch to the top, vy goes from u sin α to 0. The formula built out of exactly those pieces is (3), the one with no time in it.

H = Sy = (vy² − uy²) / (2ay) = (0² − u² sin²α) / (−2g)
maximum height H = u² sin²α / (2g)

Both minus signs cancel, which they had better — a height that came out negative would mean we had made an arithmetic slip, not that the ball went underground.

And look at the two results next to each other. The range lives on sin 2α, the height on sin²α. They peak at different angles: the range at 45°, the height at 90°. Throwing far and throwing high are two different jobs, and you cannot do both at once.

Watch out!

Every formula in this section assumes the ball lands at the same height it was launched from. Throw it off a cliff, or from your shoulder onto the ground, and the symmetry breaks — tup ≠ tdown, and these shortcuts stop being true. Go back to the two motions and run them properly — which is exactly what the next section does.

8 Thrown horizontally from a cliff

Now let us break the symmetry on purpose. Stand at the edge of a cliff of height h and throw the ball horizontally, with speed u. It lands far below you — so tup and tdown from section 6 make no sense here at all. There is no way up.

Good news: we never really needed those shortcuts. We only need the two motions.

Given u, h and g — find the time of flight t, the range Sx and the landing velocity v.

A is the launch at the edge, C is the landing. The ball starts with no vertical velocity at all.

Step one — split the launch velocity

The throw is horizontal, so α = 0:

vx = u    uy = 0

Nothing to split this time — the whole launch velocity is horizontal.

Step two — run the vertical motion from A to C

y-axis up, origin at the edge. We know uy = 0 and ay = −g, and the ball ends up h below the start: Sy = −h.

So use (1):   −h = 0 · t − g t² / 2

time of flight t = √(2h / g)

Look at what is missing from the answer: u. How hard you throw has no say in how long the ball falls. A ball dropped from the edge and a ball thrown horizontally at the same moment land at the same moment — the vertical story does not even know the horizontal one exists.

✎
Try it on a table: flick one coin off the edge and let another simply slide off at the same instant. Listen — one click, not two.

The range — from the horizontal motion

Horizontally, nothing changes: constant speed u for the whole time of flight.

range Sx = vx · t = u √(2h / g)

The landing velocity — both motions together

vx never changed, so it still equals u. vy has been growing the whole way down. From (∗):

vy = uy + ayt = −gt = −√(2gh)

The minus only says downwards. Now put the two components back together, exactly as in section 4 — Pythagoras for the size, the triangle for the direction:

landing velocity v = √(u² + 2gh)     tan β = |vy| / vx

Try it on a real one

A stone is thrown horizontally at u = 12 m/s from the top of a cliff h = 45 m high. Take g = 10 m/s².

what we findhowresult
time of flightt = √(2 · 45 / 10)3 s

landing speedv = √(12² + 30²)≈ 32.3 m/s

Read the last two rows rather than the numbers. The stone left your hand moving perfectly sideways and hits the ground at almost 70° — nearly head-first. Nothing turned it: gravity simply kept adding downward velocity for three seconds, while the sideways 12 m/s stayed exactly as it was.
