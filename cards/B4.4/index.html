The second law and entropy

or why a cup of tea never warms itself up

Leave a cup of hot tea on the table. In an hour it is at room temperature. Nobody is surprised.

Now imagine the film played backwards: a cup of lukewarm tea sits on the table, quietly takes heat out of the air of the room and comes to the boil, while the room gets a little cooler. Count the energy — every joule that enters the tea has left the air. The first law is perfectly satisfied.

And yet it never happens. Not rarely — never. So energy conservation cannot be the whole story. There must be a second rule that tells nature which direction is allowed. That rule is the second law of thermodynamics, and the quantity that keeps the score is called entropy.

1 The second law in plain words

Long before anyone spoke of entropy, the law was written down as two simple "you cannot" statements. You need to know both.

Clausius form

Heat cannot flow by itself from a colder body to a hotter one.

Kelvin form

Heat taken from a single reservoir cannot be turned completely into work in a cycle.

The Clausius form is the tea. The important words are "by itself": a refrigerator does move heat from its cold inside to the warm kitchen — but only because work is being done on it. Pull the plug and the flow goes the natural way again.

The Kelvin form is the engine of the previous card. It says that QC can never be zero: every engine must throw some heat away, so no engine has an efficiency of 100 %.

They look like two different laws, but they are one: if you could break either of them, you could build a machine that breaks the other.

2 Reversible and irreversible

The tea cooling down is an irreversible process — it runs one way only. So is a glass breaking, a drop of ink spreading in water, a gas rushing into an empty container. Every real process is like that.

A reversible process is an ideal: one that could be run backwards so that both the system and its surroundings return exactly to where they were, with no trace left. For that it must go infinitely slowly, with no friction, and heat must pass only between bodies at (almost) the same temperature. The Carnot cycle is built entirely of such steps.

No real process is truly reversible — but, like "no air resistance" in mechanics, it is the clean limit that real processes can approach and against which we measure them.

3 Entropy — the quantity that keeps the score

To turn "which way is allowed" into a calculation we need a number. That number is the entropy S. What entropy really is we will see in section 6 — first let us learn to calculate how it changes:

change in entropy at constant temperature ΔS = ΔQT

Here ΔQ is the heat given to the body, and T is its temperature in kelvin. The unit of entropy is J K−1.

  • heat goes into a body → its entropy increases
  • heat leaves a body → its entropy decreases
  • no heat exchanged (a reversible adiabatic process) → its entropy does not change

Notice the temperature in the denominator. The same 100 J of heat changes the entropy of a cold body much more than that of a hot one. Remember this — the whole second law is hiding in it.

Like the internal energy, entropy belongs to the state. So when a gas completes a cycle and returns to its starting state, its entropy returns too: ΔSgas = 0 over a full cycle.

A quick one

A block of ice of mass 0.20 kg melts at 0 °C. The specific latent heat of fusion of ice is 3.3 × 105 J kg−1. By how much does the entropy of the ice change?

The temperature stays constant while the ice melts, so the formula applies directly. The heat taken in is Q = mL = 0.20 × 3.3 × 105 = 6.6 × 104 J, and T = 273 K:

ΔS = mLT = 6.6 × 104273 ≈ +240 J K−1

Positive — heat went in. Put 0 instead of 273 into the denominator and the formula itself tells you something is wrong.

4 The second law in the language of entropy

Go back to the tea. Say 600 J of heat passes from the tea at 350 K to the room at 300 K. (The amounts are small enough that neither temperature changes noticeably.)

The same heat leaves a hot body and enters a cold one — the cold one gains more entropy than the hot one loses

The tea loses entropy, the room gains entropy — and because the room is colder, it gains more than the tea loses. The total goes up.

Now run the film backwards: 600 J from the room into the tea. The room would lose 2.0 J K−1, the tea would gain only 1.7 J K−1. The total would go down. And that is exactly what never happens:

the second law of thermodynamics ΔStotal ≥ 0

The total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. It increases in every real (irreversible) process, and stays the same only in the ideal reversible limit. The biggest isolated system there is — the system together with all its surroundings — is the universe, so you will also meet it as "the entropy of the universe never decreases".

Watch out!

The law is about the total. The entropy of one part can go down — the tea's did, and so does the entropy of water freezing in a freezer. But a part can lose entropy only if its surroundings gain at least as much. And the careful wording is "never decreases", not "always increases": for a reversible process the total change is exactly zero.

5 Back to the engine — where Carnot's formula comes from

Now we can pay a debt from the previous card. Look at what a Carnot engine does to its surroundings in one cycle. The hot reservoir gives away QH at temperature TH; the cold reservoir receives QC at TC. The gas itself has completed a cycle, so its own entropy has not changed.

entropy of the two reservoirs over one cycle ΔStotal = − QHTH + QCTC

The Carnot cycle is reversible, so this total is exactly zero:

QCTC = QHTH   →   QCQH = TCTH   →   η = 1 − QCQH = 1 − TCTH

And here is why some heat must be thrown away. Taking QH out of the hot reservoir lowers its entropy. Work carries no entropy. So the only way to keep the total from falling is to put entropy back somewhere — by dumping heat into the cold reservoir. The least you can get away with is QC = QH · TC/TH, and that is the Carnot engine. Any real engine is irreversible, makes the total entropy grow, and has to dump more.

6 So what is entropy?

So far entropy has been a recipe: heat divided by temperature. A recipe is not an understanding. What is this quantity actually measuring — and why should it only ever grow? To see it we have to look inside, at the particles. And it is easiest to start not with atoms but with coins.

Step 1 — two ways to describe the same thing

Throw four coins on the table. You can describe what you see in two ways:

  • The rough description: "two heads and two tails". It does not say which coins. This is called a macrostate — what you see from far away.
  • The full description: "coin 1 heads, coin 2 tails, coin 3 tails, coin 4 heads". This is called a microstate — every detail.

Step 2 — count the ways

One macrostate can be made in several different ways. Let us simply list all of them.

Every possible result of four coins. Each row is one microstate; each column is one macrostate.

"4 heads" can be made in only one way. "2 heads" can be made in six ways. The number of ways — the number of microstates that give the same macrostate — is written Ω (omega).

Step 3 — the definition

what entropy is Entropy is a measure of the number of ways the particles of a system, and their energy, can be arranged
without changing what the system looks like from outside.

Many ways → high entropy. Few ways → low entropy. Exactly one way → zero entropy. That is all the word means.

Boltzmann turned this sentence into a formula:

entropy and the number of microstates S = kB ln Ω

Ω is the number of ways, kB is the Boltzmann constant — it gives entropy its unit, J K−1. The logarithm is there only to tame the numbers: for a real gas Ω is unimaginably large, and ln turns it into something we can write down. What matters is the direction: when Ω grows, S grows.

For our coins: "4 heads" has Ω = 1, so S = kB ln 1 = 0. "2 heads" has Ω = 6, so S = kB ln 6 ≈ 1.8 kB.

If a system goes from a state with Ω1 ways to a state with Ω2 ways, its entropy changes by

change in entropy ΔS = kB ln Ω2 − kB ln Ω1 = kB ln Ω2Ω1

Step 4 — from coins to matter

Replace the coins by atoms, and "heads or tails" by "where each atom is and how much energy it has". The idea is the same — count the ways:

what happensthe number of waysentropy
ice meltsatoms locked in a crystal become free to move — far more arrangementsincreases
a gas spreads into a bigger volumemore places for every atom to beincreases
a body is heatedmore energy to share out between the atoms — more ways to share itincreases
water freezesatoms settle into fixed places — fewer arrangementsdecreases

People often say "entropy is disorder". That is a fair short version — a messy room can be messy in a million ways, a tidy one in very few — but when you need to be exact, say "the number of ways".

Look at the third row once more: giving heat to a body increases the number of ways. That is why the recipe of section 3, ΔS = ΔQ/T, and the counting of this section describe one and the same quantity — once seen from outside, once from inside.

Step 5 — why entropy grows

Shake the four coins and look. Every one of the 16 rows of the picture is equally likely. But six of those rows say "2 heads" and only one says "4 heads" — so you will see "2 heads" six times as often. Nothing pushes the coins towards it. There are simply more ways to be there.

With four coins the rare states still turn up now and then. With 100 coins, "all heads" is one row out of 2100 — you could shake for the whole age of the universe and not see it. And a cup of tea has about 1025 molecules.

That is the second law, seen from inside: a system left to itself drifts to the state that can be made in the most ways — and stays there. Heat flowing from the hot tea to the cool room increases the total number of ways. The reverse would decrease it — which is not forbidden, just so unlikely that it never happens.

7 Try it on a real one

Four coins lie on a tray, all showing heads. The tray is shaken, and afterwards the coins show two heads and two tails.

(a) How many microstates does each of the two macrostates have?
(b) Find the change in entropy of the coins. Give the answer in terms of kB.
(c) The tray is shaken many more times. What will the coins most probably show, and why?

One way to have four heads; six ways to have two

(a) Count the ways. Before: all four coins show heads. There is no choice to make — only one arrangement fits. Ω1 = 1.

After: two heads and two tails. Now there is a choice — which two coins show heads? Coins 1 and 2, or 1 and 3, or 1 and 4, or 2 and 3, or 2 and 4, or 3 and 4. Six possibilities. Ω2 = 6.

(b) Put the two numbers into the formula.

ΔS = kB ln Ω2Ω1 = kB ln 61 = kB ln 6 ≈ 1.8 kB

The answer is positive: the number of ways went up from 1 to 6, so the entropy went up.

(c) Use the second law. Every shake puts the coins into one of the 16 microstates at random. Six of the 16 are "2 heads" — more than for any other macrostate. So "2 heads" is the state with the highest entropy, and it is what the coins will show most often. The start, "4 heads", has the lowest entropy; it comes back only once in 16 shakes on average.

This is the pattern of every exam question on microstates: count the ways before, count the ways after, take kB ln of their ratio — and for the "comment" part, say that the system moves towards the macrostate with more microstates, because that is the one with the higher entropy.
