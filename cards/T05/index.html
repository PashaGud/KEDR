round and round we go

Circular motionwith constant speed

In the previous topics we learnt how to deal with bodies that move along a straight line. Now it is time to study bodies whose trajectory is a circle.

To make this first meeting easier, we will only look at bodies that go around the circle with the same speed all the time: not speeding up, not slowing down.

Let's try to list some examples:

  • the hands of a clock;
  • everything standing still on our planet — it still rotates together with the Earth;
  • every point on a bicycle tyre while the bike rides at constant speed;
  • a child whom a parent spins around by the hands;
  • a carousel

…and so on.

The tyre point circles around the axle. Seen from the road, its path is a very different curve!
1

Something is always pulling to the centre

Let me use my dog to show you the first property. His name is Archik — a lovely Jack Russell terrier who goes completely crazy and uncontrollable when he sees another animal.

When he is super happy — so, all the time — he likes to run around me. And I honestly do not know how he does it while keeping the same distance between us. In maths this distance is called the radius.

What I want you to think about is this: what makes him run around me? What forces him to? He is not attached to me by anything physical — no chain, no string. Between us there is nothing at all.

I can think of only one thing — love. The unseen string between every dog and its owner.

The point of this example: if a body moves along a circle, something must keep pulling it towards the centre of that circle. That pull is the reason the body keeps turning instead of running off straight.

The pull always points to the centre
Earth
circles the Sun. We picture the Sun at the centre, pulling the Earth towards itself.
Archik
runs around me, and love is what makes him turn.
A child
is spun around by a parent who keeps the child's hands in a firm grip.
Watch out!

The pull has to point to the centre, but nothing has to sit at the centre. Love may be why Archik wants to turn, but what physically pushes him sideways is his paws gripping the grass.

Same with a car on a roundabout: nothing stands in the middle. The tyres pushing against the road do the job.

2

Velocity points along the tangent

For the second property I want you to think: what happens when this attachment breaks? Imagine that I, the owner, suddenly disappear. Or the Sun disappears. Or, scarier — the father suddenly opens his grip and lets go of his child.

But I personally like another example — the Summer Olympic Games. In the hammer throw, athletes must throw a metal ball on a chain as far as possible. So the athlete spins the ball as hard as he can to give it speed, and at some point lets it go. While he holds the grip, the ball circles around him. The whole question is: at what moment to let go?

Watching on TV, we notice that athletes release the hammer when the chain is perpendicular to the direction of the throw. Let it go too late and the ball flies off to the left. Let it go too early and it flies off to the right. In both cases it lands outside the sector.

What do we conclude from this?

Once released, the ball keeps flying in the direction of its velocity at the moment of release. That is the only thing it "remembers".

Also look at the angle between the chain (the radius) and the flight path: it is 90°. Always. So for any body moving in a circle, the velocity is directed along the tangent, perpendicular to the radius.

3

Linear speed and period

I will leave my dog for a moment and show you a traditional Caucasian dance you may not know. It is called lezginka. I came across it while cycling in the centre of Moscow. I was riding slowly up a narrow street when I heard drums and music coming from inside a building. Curiosity made me stop and look through the window. It was a dance club called Adat.

A woman in a long dress was circling around a man, gliding like a swan. She moved so smoothly that she did not bob up and down at all, as if she were on roller skates.

So let's study her movement.

Who is where
O
where the man stands
A
where she was when I first saw her
B
where she was when I stopped looking
▬
her path between A and B — the arc sAB

She danced so smoothly that I can claim her speed was constant. So, just as in straight-line motion:

v=sABtAB her speed = length of arc AB ÷ time from A to B

But this formula is a bit awkward: it is not convenient to measure a piece of a circle. Let's simplify. Picture that I watched her longer — until she came back to point A. Distance and time both change, but her speed is the same. Now the distance is the whole circumference, and the time is the time of one full revolution.

Circumference of a circle = 2πr. Remember it from maths!
v=2πrT(1)

The time of one full revolution has its own name — the period, T.

And v here is an old quantity with a new name: linear speed. "Speed" is the old part — it still tells us how many metres she covers along the circle every second. "Linear" is the new part: it reminds us we are measuring along the line of the path, not counting turns.

T
Period — the time of one full revolution. Unit: second, [T] = s.
v
Linear speed — distance along the circle per unit time. Unit: m/s.

We use the period for anything that repeats itself. The second hand of a clock: T = 60 s. The Earth going around the Sun: T ≈ 365 days.

A period is always a time!

4

Angular speed

Watching the dance, I noticed another detail. While she was flying around, the man in the centre was always looking at her. His gaze always pointed exactly where she was, which means his head was rotating too to follow her.

So we can ask a new question: how fast was he turning his head?

She

moves by metres along the circle.

He

turns by degrees, or better, by radians.

Now this formula starts to make sense:

ω=θABtAB angular speed = angle turned ÷ time taken

Angular speed ω (omega) shows how fast something is turning — how many radians it turns through every second.

A full turn: 360° = 2π rad. Physics loves radians, so get used to them.

Measuring angles is also not that comfortable — we do not always carry a protractor. So we do the same trick as with linear speed and watch her for one full revolution: the angle becomes a full turn, 2π, and the time becomes the period T.

ω=2πT(2) [ω] = rad/s = 1/s
5

How v and ω are related

Put formulas (1) and (2) side by side:

v=2πrT=2πT·r ω=2πT

The piece 2π / T sits inside the first formula — and it is exactly ω. So:

v=ωr(3)

Linear speed = how fast you turn × how far you are from the centre. Turn at the same rate further out, and you move faster. Keep this thought, it comes back in section 9.

6

Period and frequency

Let's picture this situation: some body moves around a circle and makes N = 10 revolutions in t = 5 s.

N
number of revolutions
t
time of all these revolutions

How do we find the period? The period is the time of one revolution, so: time of one = time of all ÷ number of all.

T=tN In our case: T=5 s10=0.5 s

But what if a body rotates very fast — an enormous number of revolutions in a short time? For instance, N = 1000 in t = 1 min:

T=tN=60 s1000=0.06 s

The faster a body rotates, the harder it is to feel what the period means. 0.06 s says very little to us: we are not used to working with such tiny numbers, so we don't really feel them. But there is a solution! Instead of the time of one revolution, let's find how many revolutions fit into one second.

f=Nt f=100060 s≈16.7 s⁻¹ = 16.7 Hz

This is the frequency: about 17 revolutions every second. Much easier to imagine. Its unit, s⁻¹, has its own name — the hertz (Hz).

Look at the two formulas once more. T = t/N and f = N/t are the same fraction turned upside down — period and frequency are reciprocals:

T=1f f=1T

Thus, every formula with a period can be written with a frequency:

v=2πrT=2πrf ω=2πT=2πf
7

Centripetal acceleration

Centripetal means "towards the centre".

Please excuse me — I am going to borrow a little theory from a future topic, because it will make this one much clearer: acceleration is always the result of some force acting on a body.

Push the chair to the right, and it starts to speed up to the right. The push and the acceleration point the same way.

Now apply this to circular motion. We said that a body moving in a circle is always pulled towards the centre. In other words, there is a centripetal force. "Centripetal force" is just the name for that pull.

And if the force points to the centre, then it causes an acceleration that also points to the centre: the centripetal acceleration ac.

Here I see that ac ⟂ v, always. That means it is not going to change the size of the velocity.

Remember projectile motion: g pointed straight down, so it never changed vx. A push can only change velocity along its own direction.

But then what does it do? We know for sure that where there is acceleration, velocity changes. And here is the point: it changes, but not its magnitude — its direction.

Picture it with wind

Think about what this wind does to our velocity at this particular moment. Exactly — a sideways push just slightly turns the velocity without changing its magnitude. Keep the push always aimed at the centre, and the body keeps turning: that is a circle.

ac=v²r =(ωr)²r= ac=ω²r ac=4π²rT²

The first one uses v = ωr from section 5. The third one is the same thing with ω = 2π/T put in — it's handy when a problem gives you the period.

ac is sometimes called "normal acceleration". Do you know why?

Hint: in geometry, "normal" means perpendicular.

8

Tangential acceleration

In school physics you will not meet this very often, but you have to understand what it means. Let me show you with the wind again — now with a second gust blowing along the path.

ac
the wind towards the centre turns the body.
aτ
the wind along the path changes the speed: against the motion it slows the body down, with the motion it speeds it up. This is the tangential acceleration.

So aτ exists only when the speed is changing during circular motion — when the body starts to rotate faster or slower. In this card the speed is constant, so aτ = 0 and only ac is left.

9

Same ω or same v? A disc and two gears

A spinning disc: everybody shares ω

Take a spinning disc — a vinyl record, a carousel, a potter's wheel. Put two points on the same radius: point A close to the centre and point B at the edge, twice as far out. They lie on one line from the centre, and they stay on that line forever, because the disc is solid.

On a carousel the horse on the outer ring moves faster than the one near the middle, even though they go around together.

In the same time both points turn through the same angle θ, so they have the same angular speed ω. But B runs along a circle twice as big, so it covers twice the distance. From v = ωr:

ωA=ωB vB=ω·2r=2vA

Two gears: the touching point shares v

Now the opposite situation. A small gear drives a big one. Their teeth are locked together where they touch, so at that point both gears must move with the same linear speed. If one rim moved faster than the other, the teeth would grind and break.

v=ω1r1=ω2r2

Same v, different radii — so the small gear must spin faster: the smaller the gear, the bigger its ω. If the big gear is three times bigger, the small one makes three turns for every one turn of the big one. A bicycle chain works the same way: every link of the chain moves at one speed, over the big front sprocket and the small back one.

Watch out!

Before you write any equation, ask how the two things are connected.

Parts of one solid body (points on a disc, a wheel, a carousel) share ω. Things touching at a rim, or joined by a belt or chain (gears, sprockets), share v.
