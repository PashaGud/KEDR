Physics Course · Theory · B4.3 to push again, the piston first has to come back

Cycles and heat engines

or how much of the heat can be turned into work

Heat a gas under a piston and it expands and does work. Wonderful — once. The piston reaches the end of the cylinder and the show is over. An engine that pushes one time is not an engine.

To push again, the gas has to be brought back to where it started. And here is the catch: if you bring it back along the same road, you must put in exactly the work you got out. Net result — nothing.

So the whole art of engine building is one idea: come back by a cheaper road than the one you went out on. Expand the gas while it is hot and the pressure is high; compress it back while it is cold and the pressure is low. The difference is yours to keep.

1 A cycle — out and back again

A cycle is a chain of processes that returns the gas to its starting state. On a pV diagram it is a closed loop.

One fact about a cycle does half the work in every problem. The gas is back in the same state — the same p, the same V, the same T. Internal energy belongs to the state, so over one full cycle

over one complete cycle ΔU = 0   →   Qnet = Wnet

The second half is just the first law with ΔU = 0. Add up all the heat, with signs — what went in minus what came out. Add up all the work, with signs. The two totals must be equal. The gas keeps nothing for itself.

See it on a real cycle

Words like "the totals must be equal" are easy to nod at. Let us actually count. A monatomic ideal gas goes round the cycle A → B → C → A.

Heating at constant volume, expansion at constant pressure, and a straight road back

For every step we find three things with the tools of the first two cards: the work W (the area under the line), the change ΔU = 32Δ(pV), and then the heat from the first law, Q = ΔU + W. The product pV at the corners: A — 200 J, B — 600 J, C — 1200 J.

A → B, the volume is constant. No area under a vertical line: W = 0. ΔU = 32(600 − 200) = +600 J. So Q = 600 + 0 = +600 J.

B → C, the pressure is constant. W = pΔV = 3.0 × 105 × 2.0 × 10−3 = +600 J. ΔU = 32(1200 − 600) = +900 J. So Q = 900 + 600 = +1500 J.

C → A, a compression along a straight line. The area under it is a trapezium: average pressure 2.0 × 105 Pa times 2.0 × 10−3 m3 = 400 J — and the gas is compressed, so W = −400 J. ΔU = 32(200 − 1200) = −1500 J. So Q = −1500 − 400 = −1900 J.

stepΔU, JW, JQ, J
A → B+6000+600
B → C+900+600+1500
C → A−1500−400−1900
whole cycle0+200+200

Now add each column. The internal energy went up by 600, up by 900, down by 1500 — back to exactly where it was: ΔU = 0. The work adds up to 600 − 400 = 200 J. The heat adds up to 600 + 1500 − 1900 = 200 J. The same 200 J.

That is what Qnet = Wnet means: the gas took in 2100 J of heat, gave 1900 J of it back, and the 200 J that did not come back as heat came out as work.

✎
This is your free check in every cycle problem. Add the Q column, add the W column. If the two sums are not equal — as they are here, 200 and 200 — something was calculated or copied wrong.

2 The work of a cycle is the area of the loop

Work is the area under the line. On the way out — the expansion — the gas does work, and the area under the upper line is positive. On the way back — the compression — work is done on the gas, and the area under the lower line counts as negative.

Big area out, small area back — what is left is the area inside the loop

Subtract one from the other and what remains is exactly the area enclosed by the loop:

net work of a cycle Wnet = area inside the loop

The direction of the arrows decides the sign:

  • clockwise — the expansion runs along the upper line. Wnet > 0: the gas gives out work. This is an engine.
  • anticlockwise — the compression runs along the upper line. Wnet < 0: work has to be put in. This is a refrigerator or a heat pump.

3 What every heat engine does

Forget the details of the loop for a moment and look at an engine from far away. Whatever is inside — steam, petrol vapour, our ideal gas — the same three things happen in every cycle.

Heat flows from hot to cold, and the engine takes its share on the way

  • The engine takes heat QH from something hot — the hot reservoir at temperature TH: burning fuel, a reactor, the Sun.
  • It turns part of that heat into work W.
  • It throws the rest, QC, into something cold — the cold reservoir at TC: the air, a river, the sea.

The engine itself runs in a cycle, so its own ΔU is zero and the energy simply has to balance:

energy balance of an engine W = QH − QC

In this picture QH and QC are both written as positive amounts — "how much" — and the arrows show the direction.

Watch out!

The heat thrown away is not a design mistake that a clever engineer could remove. Dumping QC is the "cheap road back" — without it the gas cannot return to its start and there is no cycle. Why it can never be zero is the subject of the next card.

4 Efficiency — what you get for what you pay

You pay for QH — that is the fuel. You want W. The efficiency is the share of the heat taken in that comes out as work:

efficiency of a heat engine η = useful workheat taken in = WQH = QH − QCQH = 1 − QCQH

It is a number between 0 and 1, often given in per cent. An efficiency of 0.30 means: of every 100 J of heat taken in, 30 J become work and 70 J go to the cold reservoir.

When the cycle is drawn on a pV diagram, or given as a table of steps, the recipe is always the same:

  • W is the net work — the area of the loop, or the sum of the work of all steps with signs.
  • QH is the sum of the heat over the steps where heat goes into the gas — the positive Q only.
  • The steps where heat comes out make up QC. They are a loss, not a cost — they never go into the denominator.

5 The Carnot cycle — the best any engine can do

In 1824 a young French engineer, Sadi Carnot, asked a bold question: forget friction, forget leaks, forget every practical trouble — what is the best efficiency an engine could have in principle, working between a hot reservoir at TH and a cold one at TC?

His answer is a cycle made of the two "cleanest" processes from the previous card — two isothermal and two adiabatic.

The Carnot cycle: heat is exchanged only on the two isotherms

stepprocesswhat happens
A → Bisothermal expansion at THtakes in QH, does work
B → Cadiabatic expansionno heat; does work and cools to TC
C → Disothermal compression at TCgives out QC, work is done on it
D → Aadiabatic compressionno heat; work is done on it, warms back to TH

Why these four? Heat is taken in only at the highest temperature and given out only at the lowest; the two adiabats just carry the gas between the two temperatures with no heat passing at all. Every step can be run backwards — the cycle is reversible. For such a cycle the heats are in the same ratio as the temperatures, QC/QH = TC/TH (we will see where this comes from in the next card), and so

Carnot efficiency — temperatures in kelvin ηCarnot = 1 − TCTH

Read what this formula says, because it is one of the strongest statements in physics:

  • No engine working between the same two temperatures can beat it. A real engine is always below this number.
  • The limit depends only on the two temperatures — not on the gas, not on the size, not on the engineer.
  • To raise it, make the hot side hotter or the cold side colder. That is why power stations run their steam as hot as the metal allows.
  • It is never 1. That would need TC = 0 K — and nothing can be used as a reservoir at absolute zero.

Watch out!

There are two efficiency formulas, and they are not the same thing. η = W/QH works for any engine. η = 1 − TC/TH is only for the Carnot cycle — for a real engine it gives the ceiling, not the actual value. And it needs kelvin: 27 °C and 327 °C are 300 K and 600 K, so η = 0.50, not 0.92.

6 Try it on a real one

A monatomic ideal gas is taken round the cycle ABCDA shown on the diagram. Find the net work of one cycle, the heat taken in, and the efficiency. Then compare with the Carnot limit for the same highest and lowest temperatures.

Two isovolumetric steps and two isobaric steps, run clockwise

Signs first. The loop is clockwise, so it is an engine: Wnet > 0.

Net work. The loop is a rectangle: Wnet = Δp · ΔV = (3.0 − 1.0) × 105 × (3.0 − 1.0) × 10−3 = 400 J.

Heat, step by step. For each step we need ΔU = 32Δ(pV) and W, then Q = ΔU + W. First the product pV at each corner: A — 100 J, B — 300 J, C — 900 J, D — 300 J.

The bottom row is the check from section 1: ΔU adds up to zero, and the net heat equals the net work — and equals the area we found.

Efficiency. Heat goes in on A → B and B → C: QH = 300 + 1500 = 1800 J.

η = WnetQH = 4001800 ≈ 0.22

The Carnot limit. T = pV/(nR), so the gas is hottest where pV is largest — at C — and coldest at A. Then TC/TH = 100/900, and ηCarnot = 1 − 19 ≈ 0.89.

Our rectangle turns 22 % of its heat into work where a Carnot engine between the same temperatures would turn 89 %. The reason is in the table: the rectangle takes heat in all the way from the coldest corner up to the hottest, and throws heat out all the way down. Carnot's cycle takes heat only at the very top temperature and gives it out only at the very bottom.
