Four processes

or what the first law turns into when you hold one thing still

The first law has three players: Q = ΔU + W. In a general process all three are alive at once, and that is hard to think about. So physicists do what they always do — they look for the simple cases first.

There are four of them. In each one, something is held fixed — the volume, the pressure, the temperature, or the heat — and because of that one term of the first law either vanishes or becomes easy. Learn these four and you can read almost any pV diagram, because exam cycles are built out of exactly these pieces.

The four roads on a pV diagram. The shaded area is the work done by the gas.

We keep the agreements of the previous card: a fixed amount of monatomic ideal gas, Q counted into the gas, W done by the gas. And we keep two tools in the pocket for every section below: pV = nRT and ΔU = 32nRΔT.

1 Isovolumetric — the piston is locked

Lock the piston, or simply take a closed rigid bottle. The volume cannot change. On the pV diagram this is a vertical line.

No change of volume means no area under the line — the gas pushes on the walls, but nothing moves, so it does no work:

constant volume W = 0   →   Q = ΔU

Every joule of heat you give goes straight into internal energy — the gas has nothing else to do with it. Heat in: the temperature and the pressure go up, the line goes up. Heat out: the line goes down.

2 Isobaric — the piston is free

Let the piston move freely with a constant load on it — the atmosphere, or a weight. The pressure of the gas then stays equal to that load. On the diagram this is a horizontal line.

This is the one process where all three players are alive, but each of them is easy:

constant pressure W = pΔV,    ΔU = 32pΔV,    Q = ΔU + W = 52pΔV

Where the middle one comes from: at constant pressure pV = nRT gives pΔV = nRΔT, and we put that into ΔU = 32nRΔT. We did exactly this in the example of the previous card.

Look at how the heat is shared. Out of every 5 parts of heat, 3 stay in the gas and 2 leave as work. Heating a gas at constant pressure is therefore more "expensive" than at constant volume: for the same rise in temperature you also have to pay for the expansion.

3 Isothermal — the temperature is held

Put the cylinder in good contact with something big at a fixed temperature — a water bath, the air of the room — and move the piston slowly, so that the gas always has time to exchange heat and stay at that temperature.

Constant T means pV = nRT = constant. The line is a curve that falls as the volume grows — double the volume, half the pressure. Such a curve is called an isotherm.

And constant T means the internal energy does not change at all:

constant temperature ΔU = 0   →   Q = W

The gas is a perfect middleman here. In an expansion it takes heat in and hands all of it on as work, keeping nothing. In a compression the work done on it leaves at once as heat.

Watch out!

"Isothermal" does not mean "no heat". It is the opposite: heat must flow, otherwise the temperature could not stay constant while the gas does work. The process with no heat is the next one.

The work is still the area under the curve. The curve is not straight, so in the exam you count the squares of the grid — or you are simply given the value.

4 Adiabatic — no heat at all

Now do the opposite of the water bath: wrap the cylinder in insulation — or just move the piston so fast that heat has no time to get in or out. This is the bicycle pump and the air rushing out of the tyre.

no heat exchanged Q = 0   →   ΔU = −W

The gas has only its own internal energy to pay with. If it expands and does work, W > 0, so ΔU < 0 — it cools. If it is compressed, W < 0, so ΔU > 0 — it heats up. No flame needed.

Along this road all three of p, V and T change together. For a monatomic ideal gas they are tied by one rule:

adiabatic process, monatomic ideal gas pV5/3 = constant   →   p1V15/3 = p2V25/3

This one you do not derive — it is given in the data booklet, and you only need to use it. If the question asks about temperature instead of pressure, bring in pV = nRT: replace p by nRT/V and the rule becomes TV2/3 = constant.

The work comes from the first law, with no area to count: W = −ΔU = −32nRΔT. And since nRT = pV at both ends,

work in an adiabatic process W = 32(p1V1 − p2V2)
✎
On the calculator the power 5/3 needs brackets: (V₁ ÷ V₂) ^ (5 ÷ 3). And the units of volume cancel in the ratio — you may leave both volumes in cm³ or in 10⁻³ m³.

5 Isotherm or adiabat — telling them apart

Both are falling curves, and on an exam diagram they often sit next to each other. Start both from the same state A and let the gas expand to the same final volume.

From the same point, the adiabat always falls more steeply than the isotherm

Why steeper? On the isotherm the pressure falls for one reason only: the volume grows. On the adiabat it falls for two reasons at once: the volume grows and the gas cools. So at the same final volume the adiabatic gas ends at a lower pressure — on a colder isotherm.

For a compression read the same picture backwards: the adiabat climbs more steeply, because the gas is also heating up.

✎
When a question asks "show that BC is adiabatic" or "which part is isothermal", do not judge by eye. Take two points of the curve from the graph and test them: is pV the same (isothermal), or is pV^(5/3) the same (adiabatic)?

7 Try it on a real one

A monatomic ideal gas at a pressure of 3.2 × 105 Pa and a volume of 1.0 × 10−3 m3 expands adiabatically to a volume of 8.0 × 10−3 m3. Find the final pressure, the work done by the gas, and how many times its temperature has dropped.

What we know

p1 = 3.2 × 105 Pa

V1 = 1.0 × 10−3 m3

V2 = 8.0 × 10−3 m3

The signs first

adiabatic → Q = 0

expands → W > 0

so ΔU < 0: the gas cools

Pressure. From p1V15/3 = p2V25/3:

p2 = p1 (V1/V2)5/3 = 3.2 × 105 × (1/8)5/3 = 3.2 × 105 × 132 = 1.0 × 104 Pa

The volume grew 8 times, the pressure fell 32 times. On an isotherm it would have fallen only 8 times — the extra factor of 4 is the cooling.

Work. p1V1 = 3.2 × 105 × 1.0 × 10−3 = 320 J and p2V2 = 1.0 × 104 × 8.0 × 10−3 = 80 J, so

W = 32(p1V1 − p2V2) = 32 × (320 − 80) = 360 J

Temperature. T = pV/(nR), so T2/T1 = p2V2/(p1V1) = 80/320 = 14. The absolute temperature dropped 4 times — the same factor of 4 we met in the pressure.

The gas did 360 J of work and received nothing from outside. It paid for all of it with its own internal energy: ΔU = −360 J.
