Motion with constant speed / velocity

Now, let’s learn by examples! ↘

Constant
means it is not changing with time.
Speed
means how many meters you travel per second. scalar — one piece of information: how fast it goes.
Velocity
means a vector with length equal to speed. vector — two pieces of information: how fast, and where.
1

Watching a body move

I’m starting to look at a body from t = 0 — the moment I turn my stopwatch on.

Pay attention — during equal time intervals we travel the same distance! That is motion with constant speed.

How to find the speed?

Way 1 — just look

For each interval of one second we travel 5 m each time, so

v = 5 m/s
Way 2 — the formula

Speed is distance traveled over time taken:

v = St = 15 m3 s = 5 m/s
So, from this example we get: S = v · t where here S means distance, and v — speed.
Still no direction here! This works when you travel with constant speed — otherwise we have to add the word “average”. More about it later.
2

Now direction matters

Same body, same dots — but this time we put an axis underneath and choose a positive direction.

xf — final coordinate xi — initial coordinate
Now I choose myself the positive direction — to the left.
I’m starting to pay attention to where we go, not only how fast.
Since I’m thinking about direction, I say: velocity = −5 m/s. Minus means: opposite to the positive direction.
So, from this example we get: S = v · t v = displacementtime = −15 m3 s = −5 m/s

where here S means displacement, and v — velocity.

In the 1st formula “direction counting” has turned off, while in the second one it is on!

So please, always ask yourself: do I consider the direction or not?
3

A weird one — motion on a plane

What do we see here? The body moves on a plane — both x and y are changing. Looks complicated… but I know what to do: bring in some friends.
A

Artur looks along the x-axis. From where he stands, only y seems to change.

A

Asad looks up along the y-axis. He only sees x changing.

Therefore

We need to split our velocity:

v̄ normal / ordinary velocity vx x-component of v̄ — in simple words, how fast Asad sees the body moving vy y-component of v̄ — in simple words, how fast Artur sees the body moving
So: vx= v · cos α = 3 m/s · cos(30°) ≈ 2.6 m/s
So: vy= v · sin α = 3 m/s · sin(30°) = 1.5 m/s
Now let’s say, that the body will travel for t = 10 s.
How far Asad will see the body travel?
Sx = vx · t ≈ 2.6 m/s · 10 s = 26 m.
But for Artur
Sy = vy · t = 1.5 m/s · 10 s = 15 m.
4

From displacement to coordinates

What if I go further in this formula (S = v t)?
where S — displ., v — velocity, t — time

Instead of „S” I will consider how coordinates were changing.
knowing that S = xf − xi
I can rewrite the formula:
xf − xi = v t  ⇒  xf = xi + v t
xf(t) = xi + v · t
where xf(t) final coordinate — depends on time, or: tends to change with time when the object is moving xi initial coordinate (it is usually some picked / fixed value — the point / position when we start to look at the body) v velocity (has direction in it)

How do people visualise motion?

people → physicists & ordinary ones — they see it differently!

Let’s start from normal people. They just try to picture the moving body in real life, in several positions. For instance — our first body from this scene →
body 1 — 5 m every second
But physicists like to use mathematical graphs for that. It is another way of looking at the world!

From this graph we see that the line never goes down — because it is distance: it can only grow.

Let’s spice it up — a second body, a bit faster

The ordinary person will claim that the second body moves faster, as it travels more per 1 sec.
But the physicist will just make another line for the mathematical representation of a second body!
That is what the physicist sees. By the slope of this graph he gets that the second body moves faster.
And we still remember that here we do not consider the direction:
v₁ = 5 m/s, v₂ = 10 m/s — speed
A bridge from maths

In the maths branch we met the simplest straight-line equation, y = m·x, where m is the slope — in plain words, the thing that tells how fast the line rises or falls.

Now hold it next to the equation we have here, S = v·t. Because v is constant, it plays exactly the role m did before. Only the names changed — y became S, and x became t. That is why it is always fair to read velocity as the slope of the line.

Now, let’s pick some positive direction — to the left

1st and 2nd move to the right — opposite to our positive direction; the 3rd goes to the left, along it!

Not much has changed compared to the „distance–time” graph. Just one important thing: we also want to know the direction.
„Minus sign” tells us that we go opposite to the positive direction, and slope still tells that 2nd is faster. The 3rd has a plus — it goes along the positive direction:
v₁ = −5 m/s, v₂ = −10 m/s, v₃ = +5 m/s — velocity
A bridge from maths

There is a maths reading here too. It is still the same straight line — y = m·x, or S = v·t — only now the sign of the slope carries the direction. For the 1st and 2nd bodies m and v are negative, so their lines slope downward; for the 3rd body v is positive, so its line slopes upward.

And finally — what if we look precisely at the positions / coordinates?

Every line starts at its xᵢ and shifts by v meters each second — down if v is negative, up if positive…
That is exactly our formula from part 4: xf = xᵢ + v · t — drawn as a picture!
A bridge from maths

This time the line does not have to start from the origin, so we need the full form of a straight line, y = m·x + b. Set beside what we have, xf = xi + v·t, the match is exact: the slope m is the velocity v, and the intercept b is just the starting coordinate xi.

And what about speed and velocity themselves?

Speed–time

Flat lines above zero — speed has no direction, it is just “how fast”.

Velocity–time

Flat lines above and below zero — the sign keeps the direction in the picture.

Graphs at a glance
Distance – time

Distance–time. Always from zero, never dips. The steeper line moves faster.

Coordinate – time

Coordinate–time. Different start heights (even negative), rising or falling – the slope is the velocity.

Velocity – time

Velocity–time. Flat lines. Above zero = one way, below zero = the opposite way.

Displacement – time

Displacement–time. Straight lines from zero, up or down. Sign and slope show direction and speed.

Speed – time

Speed–time. Flat lines, always above zero – speed has no direction.

The gradient of the S–t line is the speed. Gradient positive → goes up!

Key idea

Every single graph on this page shows the same kind of motion: a body moving in a straight line at constant speed / velocity. That is exactly why each one comes out as a straight line — a steady rate always draws straight. The moment the speed starts to change, these lines will start to bend … but that is a story for the next scene.

7

Why do we sometimes use the word “average”?

Just as always, when people deal with complex situations — they simplify! Simplification means splitting the complex problem into simple ones. And we take exactly the same approach with imperfect motion — I mean, when speed / velocity isn’t constant. Let me show you!

Imagine that you travel by bus from Riga to Kabul, and it takes you exactly 10 days. It is a long, tiring drive — with lots of pee-stops, refuelling breaks, and visits to famous places on the road, like Moscow. When you finally arrive to Kabul, one gorgeous kid with the most beautiful eyes you have ever seen asks you: “How fast was the bus driving?” And at this exact moment you fall into your thoughts… what should I say? Maybe the maximum speed? Or something in between?

We precisely know

a) time of the drive — 10 days

b) distance — 5000 km

We don’t know precisely

a) time of the small breaks — who on earth counts that?

b) speeds at each moment of this drive — but we know they were different.

Thus:

Let’s have an imaginary (not real) bus, that makes no stops, never changes its speed — just goes smoothly. And it also takes 10 days to travel 5000 km.

And its speed we can find: v = St = 5000 km10 days = 500 km/day

And this thing is called “average speed”. And this is exactly what the kid is asking you about!

Watch out!

“Average speed” does not count direction, so it is always positive.

But there is also an “average velocity”, that can be found in a similar way — only now the direction counting is turned on again: instead of distance we take the displacement.

vaver = displacementtime
So “average” is just our honest simplification: we replace the messy real trip with one imaginary smooth trip that covers the same ground in the same time.
