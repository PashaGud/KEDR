Physics Course · Theory · B4.1 energy never appears and never vanishes — it only changes hands

The first law of thermodynamics

or where the energy of a gas comes from and where it goes

Pump up a bicycle tyre quickly and touch the end of the pump. It is warm. Nobody heated it — there was no flame, no sunshine, no hot water. All you did was push. And yet the air inside got hotter.

Now do the opposite: let the air rush out of the tyre and hold your finger in the stream. It is cold. The air pushed its way out — and paid for it.

These two little experiments already hold the whole card. A gas has an energy of its own. There are exactly two ways to change it: heat it, or push it. The first law is simply the honest bookkeeping of these two.

Through this whole topic we make one agreement: the gas is a monatomic ideal gas, closed in a cylinder under a piston. Single atoms, no forces between them, nothing leaks out. Everything you know from the gas laws — pV = nRT first of all — stays with us.

1 Internal energy — what the gas keeps for itself

Atoms of an ideal gas do not attract or repel each other, so they have no potential energy to speak of. All they have is motion. Add up the kinetic energy of every atom in the cylinder and you get the internal energy U of the gas.

From the kinetic model you already know that one atom carries, on average, 32kBT. Multiply by the number of atoms:

internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas U = 32 N kB T = 32 n R T = 32 p V

The last form is the same formula: for an ideal gas nRT = pV. So the internal energy can be read straight off a pV diagram — it is one and a half times the product of the two coordinates of the point.

Read this formula slowly, because it says something strong: for a fixed amount of gas, U is fixed by the state the gas is in right now — not by what happened to it an hour ago. Tell me T and I will tell you U; or tell me p and V — it is the same information, because pV = nRT.

Pressure and volume enter only through their product. Squeeze a gas to half its volume while its pressure doubles: pV is the same, T is the same, and U has not changed at all. Change pV — and you have changed T and U together.

So a change of internal energy is always a change of temperature — or, what is the same thing, a change of the product pV:

change in internal energy ΔU = 32 n R ΔT = 32 Δ(pV) = 32 (p2V2 − p1V1)
  • the gas gets hotter → ΔU > 0
  • the gas gets colder → ΔU < 0
  • the temperature comes back to where it was → ΔU = 0, whatever happened in between
✎
Keep the last line. Half of the cycle problems later are solved by it alone: the gas returned to its starting state — so its internal energy returned too.

Watch out!

In U = 32nRT the temperature is in kelvin. Heating a gas from 50 °C to 150 °C does not triple its internal energy: in kelvin it goes from 323 K to 423 K — about 1.3 times. A change ΔT is the same number in °C and in K; a temperature T itself is not.

2 Work — when the gas pushes

Let the gas expand a little. It presses on the piston with a force and the piston moves — a force acting along a displacement. That is work, the same work as in mechanics.

The gas pushes the piston a distance s. The volume grows by A·s.

Keep the pressure constant while the piston moves. The force of the gas on the piston is F = pA, the piston moves a distance s, so

work done by the gas at constant pressure W = F·s = p·A·s = p ΔV

The sign takes care of itself through ΔV = Vfinal − Vinitial:

what happensΔVWin words
the gas expands++work is done by the gas
the gas is compressed−−work is done on the gas
the volume does not change00no work at all

In this course — and in every IB problem — W means the work done by the gas. If the question says "200 J of work is done on the gas", you write W = −200 J.

3 Work is an area

Draw the process on a graph of pressure against volume — a pV diagram. Every point of it is one state of the gas; a line is a process, and an arrow on the line says which way it goes.

At constant pressure the line is horizontal, and pΔV is height times width — the area of the rectangle under it. That is not a coincidence, and it does not stop being true when the pressure changes:

The work done by the gas is the area between the line and the V-axis

So on any pV diagram: work = area under the line. Arrow to the right — the gas expands, the area counts as positive. Arrow to the left — compression, the same area counts as negative. A vertical line has no area under it: the volume did not change and no work was done.

When the line is straight, the area is a rectangle, a triangle or a trapezium. When it is curved, count the squares of the grid — that is exactly what the exam expects.

The road matters

Take a gas from state A to state B along two different roads.

Same start, same finish — different work

The start and the finish are the same, so the temperatures at A and at B are the same on both roads — and ΔU is the same. But the areas are clearly different, so the work is different.

That is the difference between the two kinds of quantities in this topic. U belongs to a state: it only cares where the gas is. W (and, as we are about to see, Q) belongs to a process: it cares how the gas got there. A gas "has" internal energy. It never "has" work or heat.

4 The first law — the bookkeeping

Back to the bicycle pump. Its air got hotter with no heating at all — work alone raised U. Put a closed bottle of air in the sun and it gets hotter with no pushing at all — heat alone raised U. Two doors into the same room.

Heat Q is energy that passes between the gas and its surroundings because their temperatures are different. Work W is energy that passes because something moved — the piston.

Energy is conserved, so the heat that enters has only two places to go:

the first law of thermodynamics Q = ΔU + W

The formula is short; the signs are where all the marks are lost. Fix them once:

positivenegative
Qheat goes into the gasheat leaves the gas
Wthe gas expands — work done by the gasthe gas is compressed — work done on it
ΔUthe gas gets hotterthe gas gets colder
✎
Before any numbers, write three little signs next to the problem: is Q +, − or 0? Is W +, − or 0? Is ΔU +, − or 0? Two of them you can usually read straight from the words. The first law hands you the third.

Check it on the pump. You compress the air quickly — too quickly for heat to leak out, so Q ≈ 0. Work is done on the gas, so W < 0. The first law: ΔU = Q − W = 0 − (negative) > 0. The air gets hotter. And the air escaping from the tyre: Q ≈ 0 again, but now the gas does the pushing, W > 0, so ΔU < 0 — cold.

Watch out!

Heat is not temperature. A gas can get hotter with Q = 0 (the pump), and it can take in heat while its temperature stays exactly the same — if it spends all of that heat on work. What the temperature follows is ΔU, not Q.

5 Try it on a real one

A cylinder holds a monatomic ideal gas at a constant pressure of 2.0 × 105 Pa. The gas is heated slowly and expands from 1.0 × 10−3 m3 to 3.0 × 10−3 m3. Find the work done by the gas, the change in its internal energy and the heat it received.

What we know

p = 2.0 × 105 Pa = const

V1 = 1.0 × 10−3 m3

V2 = 3.0 × 10−3 m3

The signs first

expands → W > 0

pV grows → T grows → ΔU > 0

so Q > 0, and bigger than both

Work. The pressure is constant, so W = pΔV = 2.0 × 105 × (3.0 − 1.0) × 10−3 = 400 J.

Internal energy. We need ΔU = 32nRΔT, but nobody gave us n or T. They are not needed: ΔU = 32Δ(pV), and at constant pressure Δ(pV) = pΔV. So

ΔU = 32 p ΔV = 32 × 400 = 600 J

Heat. The first law: Q = ΔU + W = 600 + 400 = 1000 J.

Read the result rather than the numbers. Of every 1000 J of heat, the gas kept 600 J to get hotter and spent 400 J pushing the piston. All three signs match what we predicted before calculating — if they had not, we would know where to look for the mistake.

✎
Here p was constant, so Δ(pV) = pΔV. On any other line use the general form ΔU = 3/2 (p₂V₂ − p₁V₁) — read p and V at the start and at the end, and you never need n or T.

6 What to carry out of this card

  • U = 32nRT = 32pV — the internal energy is fixed by the state: by T (in kelvin), or equally by the product pV.
  • W = pΔV at constant pressure; in general, the area under the line on the pV diagram. Expansion +, compression −.
  • Q = ΔU + W, with Q counted into the gas and W done by the gas.
  • ΔU depends only on the start and the finish; Q and W depend on the road.
